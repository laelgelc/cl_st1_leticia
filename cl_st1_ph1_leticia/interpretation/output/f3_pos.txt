This pole is clearly driven by human conversational speech, and it encodes a highly interactive, disfluent, “on‑the‑fly” spoken discourse style.

### 1. Who drives this pole?

Mean scores:
- Human: 3.38  
- Profiled GPT: 1.41  
- Unprofiled GPT: 1.39  

Humans are substantially higher than both LLM conditions. So this pole is primarily a *human conversational* dimension, only weakly approximated by GPT output.

### 2. Linguistic profile of the pole

Top loadings (all strongly speech‑like, informal, and interactional):

- Disfluencies / hesitation markers:  
  - er (.73), erm (.69), wo (.57 – likely truncated “won’t/wouldn’t”), oh (.39)  
- Informal spoken forms:  
  - wanna (.70), cos (.42), gonna (.38), okay (.47), alright (.46)  
- Interactive / addressee‑oriented items:  
  - you (.37), know (.31), say (.31), yes (.41), somebody (.41), fact (.35)

Taken together, these are classic markers of:
- Real‑time planning and repair (er, erm, oh, wo)  
- Casual, colloquial register (wanna, gonna, cos, okay, alright)  
- Direct engagement with a hearer and co‑construction of meaning (you know, you see / I say, yes, somebody)

They point to spontaneous, informal, listener‑oriented talk.

### 3. What the excerpts show

All 10 high‑scoring texts are human turns. Across them we see:

**a. Heavy disfluency and real‑time planning**

- “like oh erm dinner at for my birthday let me know if you can make it…”  
- “er er er”  
- “erm so we didn’t have sick pay at that point but I I sat down…”  
- “I was like I was like oh alright okay and then er and then a little while later…”

Speakers are clearly thinking aloud, revising, and stalling. This is not pre‑planned narrative; it’s emergent talk.

**b. Strong addressee orientation and co‑construction**

Frequent “you” and “you know” used not just referentially but as discourse devices:

- “do you know what I mean?”  
- “you know they've got these fifteen sixteen divisions…”  
- “you know what I mean?”  
- “like you say about passing things on”  
- “okay? so what's the outcome of talking to his lecturer…”

These invite alignment, check understanding, and build shared ground.

**c. Storytelling and stance‑taking in everyday situations**

The content is often anecdotal, evaluative, and interpersonal:

- Complaining about a friend’s behaviour (Excerpt 1)  
- Telling a bureaucratic/planning‑permission story (Excerpt 2)  
- Explaining work roles and organisational problems (Excerpt 3)  
- Narratives about illness at work, pay, holidays, ski trips, salary negotiations, etc.

The discourse is not just transactional; it’s rich in stance, evaluation, and interpersonal positioning (“it’s really bad”, “oh god okay I need to tell everybody”, “I was just a disaster on the slopes”).

**d. Informal, collaborative, “chatty” organisation**

Markers like “okay”, “alright”, “oh”, “yes” punctuate the discourse, signalling:

- Turn management and topic shifts (“okay this case study may not come university but…”)  
- Alignment and encouragement (“oh that's good then at least you're seeing him”)  
- Back‑channel‑like agreement within monologic stretches (“yes yes labels yes…”)

Even when only one speaker is transcribed, the language simulates dialogic interaction.

### 4. Integrating loadings and examples

Loadings tell us this pole is defined by:
- Disfluency + colloquial forms + second‑person engagement.

The examples confirm that these features cluster in:
- Spontaneous, informal, highly interactive storytelling and explanation, where the speaker:
  - Thinks aloud (er/erm, restarts, repairs),
  - Directly orients to a hearer (you, you know, okay?, yes),
  - Uses casual forms (wanna, gonna, cos, oh, alright).

This is not just “spoken vs written”; it’s specifically *chatty, co‑constructed, everyday spoken narrative/explanation*.

### 5. Possible labels for this pole

Any label should capture:  
– spontaneity and disfluency,  
– informality/colloquialism,  
– strong addressee orientation and interpersonal involvement,  
– human‑driven conversational style.

Plausible labels:

1. **Spontaneous, Addressee‑Engaged Conversation**  
   - Highlights real‑time production (“spontaneous”) and the centrality of “you/you know/okay/yes” as engagement devices.

2. **Disfluent, Colloquial Storytelling and Explanation**  
   - Emphasises the heavy “er/erm/oh” profile plus the narrative/explanatory content in the excerpts.

3. **Interactive, Chatty Spoken Mode**  
   - Captures the informal, “chatty” feel and the strong interactional orientation.

If a single label is needed, I would propose:

> **“Spontaneous, Addressee‑Engaged Conversational Talk”**

because it best integrates:
- the disfluency and on‑the‑fly planning (spontaneous),
- the direct orientation to the hearer (addressee‑engaged),
- and the overall register (conversational talk),  
and it aligns with the fact that this pole is strongly driven by human turns rather than GPT output.
