Interpretation of Factor 1 (positive pole)

1. Which group drives this pole?

- Mean scores:
  - Human: -1.27  
  - Unprofiled GPT: -2.53  
  - Profiled GPT: -2.57  

All three groups are on the negative side overall, but humans are *least* negative. In other words, humans are closest to the positive pole and therefore drive the positive end of this factor. The LLM outputs (both profiled and unprofiled) are further away from the positive pole and cluster more strongly at the negative end.

So: the positive pole is a **human‑dominated** discourse style that LLMs largely fail to reproduce.

2. Linguistic loadings

Positive loadings:
- um (1.74) – very strong
- er (.41)
- fact (.35)
- wanna (.30)

This is a very “tight” factor: it is essentially picking out a cluster of **disfluency markers and highly colloquial forms**:
- Filled pauses: um, er
- Informal contraction: wanna
- Metadiscursive / stance-like item: fact (often in “the fact that…”)

Given that the corpus is conversational, these are classic markers of **spontaneous, online speech production** and **informal, interactional stance**.

3. Evidence from the high‑scoring excerpts

All the high‑scoring texts are human turns with multiple “um” tokens. The excerpts show:

- Frequent filled pauses:
  - “but um things like the Bluetooth setting…”
  - “I know ironic um but yeah I dunno…”
  - “you can then focus on your teaching to help lea um students learn um…”
  - “you know um sta started rioting…”
  - “you get good ideas going round um garden centres round the um the kitchen gardens…”
  - “you could probably do something like that um you could do um say talking about…”
  - “and um and then um she w then we were like…”
  - “along the wall um they're a very good idea… most of our apples this year are from um were from the tree…”

- Other classic features of spontaneous talk co‑occur:
  - Self‑repairs and restarts: “sta started”, “she w then we were like”, “the the fact that a teacher a as a teacher…”
  - Vague and informal lexis: “dunno”, “like”, “sort of”, “yeah”, “you know”, “loads of”, “stuff”.
  - Long, loosely structured turns with chaining conjunctions: “and”, “but”, “so”, “cos”.
  - Narrative and explanatory discourse: recounting events, explaining processes, giving advice, reflecting on opinions.

The negative examples (t000007, t000001) are not high on the positive pole and do not contain the positive loading items; they are included only to show contrast and are not central to the positive‑pole interpretation.

4. Discourse characteristics of the positive pole

Taking the loadings and examples together, the positive pole encodes a discourse style with these properties:

- **High spontaneity / online planning**
  - Heavy use of “um”, “er” as speakers plan what to say next.
  - Frequent self‑corrections and restarts.
  - Long, syntactically loose stretches of talk.

- **Informal, conversational register**
  - “wanna”, “dunno”, “yeah”, “you know”, “like”.
  - Personal narratives, everyday topics (health, hobbies, gardening, dreams, teaching practice).

- **Interactive, subjective stance**
  - “I think”, “I figure”, “I know”, “you can see”, “you get…”.
  - “the fact that…” as a stance/packaging device for evaluations and explanations.

- **Human‑typical disfluency**
  - The factor is almost a proxy for “sounds like a real person thinking aloud”.
  - LLM outputs, even when prompted to be conversational, typically underproduce “um/er” and similar disfluencies, which explains their much lower mean scores.

5. Possible labels for this positive pole

Any label should capture:
- spontaneity and online planning,
- informality and interactional stance,
- and its association with human speech.

Plausible labels (for this pole only):

1. **Spontaneous Disfluent Conversation**
   - Justification: “um”, “er”, self‑repairs, and loosely structured turns are central; this is the most direct description of what the factor is picking up.

2. **Informal, Online‑Planned Talk**
   - Justification: highlights both register (informal) and production mode (online planning signalled by filled pauses).

3. **Human‑like Hesitant Narrative/Explanatory Discourse**
   - Justification: the excerpts are largely narratives and explanations, delivered with human‑typical hesitation markers; this distinguishes them from the more polished, LLM‑style outputs.

If I had to choose a single label for analytic use, I would opt for:

> **Spontaneous Disfluent Conversation**

because it is:
- tightly grounded in the loadings (“um”, “er”, “wanna”) and
- clearly exemplified in the excerpts (long, informal, hesitating turns),
and it captures the fact that this pole is driven by **human, not LLM**, production.
