This negative pole is best understood as a discourse of **informal, male‑focused narrative about specific episodes in time**, with a strong flavour of spontaneous, colloquial storytelling.

Below I propose labels and justify them from both the loadings and the examples, and then note which group drives the *opposite* pole (since all high‑scoring texts here are human).

---

## 1. What drives this pole?

### Source scores

Mean scores on Factor 2 (overall, not pole‑specific):

- Human: 0.24  
- Profiled GPT: 0.67  
- Unprofiled GPT: 0.63  

So GPT texts tend to be *higher* on Factor 2; humans are *lower*. Since we are interpreting the **negative** pole, this pole is **driven by human turns**: human texts are more likely to have negative scores on this factor than GPT texts.

All 10 high‑scoring negative examples are human.

## 2. Linguistic loadings: what characterises this pole?

Negative loadings (strongest to weakest):

- shit (−.39)
- um (−.39)
- alright (−.38)
- minute (−.36)
- he (−.34)
- him (−.31)
- awful (−.30)
- oh (−.30)

Key patterns:

1. **Male third‑person reference**  
   - he, him: sustained talk *about a male person* (partner, friend, teacher, baby, ex, etc.).

2. **Temporal granularity / episode timing**  
   - minute: “five minutes”, “ten or fifteen minutes”, “forty minutes later”, “twenty minutes out of town” – narrators anchor events in short, concrete time spans.

3. **Spontaneous, disfluent, colloquial style**  
   - um, oh, alright: classic markers of **online planning** and **informal stance** in conversation.
   - shit, awful: **colloquial evaluation**, mild taboo, negative affect.

Overall, the loadings point to **informal, evaluative, time‑anchored storytelling about a male figure**, produced in a very conversational, disfluent style.

## 3. Evidence from the example excerpts

All examples are human conversational narratives. Key recurring features:

### a. Focus on a male protagonist (“he / him”)

Every excerpt centres on “he / him”:

- A man telling his life story over coffee (t001327).
- A man with “a new way of thinking” and letters to the speaker (t001230).
- A male colleague/partner who “needs to grow up” (t000625).
- A baby boy’s sleep and nap routine (t000292).
- A talkative male classmate/teacher (t000172).
- A male friend with “news” and scheduling around him (t000548).
- A partner on shifts with a noisy vehicle (t000009).
- A drunk male hockey lad being looked after (t000552).
- A male teacher and a boy in class (t000551).
- A violent ex‑boyfriend (t001383).

The discourse is consistently **third‑person narrative about a specific male individual**, often in relational or caregiving contexts.

### b. Fine‑grained timing and short episodes (“minute”)

“minute” appears in multiple ways:

- “ten or fifteen minutes he said oh do you want a coffee” (t001327)
- “ten minutes later so I don’t have to come” (t001230)
- “I don’t believe for one minute that he’s being malicious” (idiomatic, but still time‑lexeme; t000625)
- “forty minutes later so it was only a quick nap” (t000292)
- “five minutes not even joking five minutes just chatting on about nothing” (t000172)
- “twenty minutes out of town” (t001383)

Speakers **segment and pace the story** with short, concrete time spans, giving a sense of lived, real‑time experience.

### c. Informal, disfluent, “live” conversational style

Across excerpts:

- Repetitions and repairs: “it started it it star it he he was getting into it” (t001327); “he he’s up at half five” (t000009).
- Fillers and hesitations: “erm”, “you know”, “like”, “I mean”.
- Colloquial intensifiers and stance: “literally”, “not even joking”, “blah blah blah blah blah”, “all that sorta shit”, “he were a violent shit”.
- Evaluative adjectives: “malicious”, “wicked teacher”, “violent shit”, “awful” (from loadings).

This is **unplanned, highly interactive spoken narrative**, not polished or expository.

### d. Everyday interpersonal and emotional content

The stories are about:

- Relationships and conflict (ex‑boyfriend swinging for her; ignoring letters; being ignored by friends).
- Care and responsibility (looking after a drunk friend; managing a baby’s sleep; planning around someone’s schedule).
- Judging others’ behaviour (he “needs to grow up”; “violent shit”; “wicked teacher”).

So the discourse is **interpersonal, evaluative, and emotionally coloured**, but still grounded in concrete, everyday events.

---

## 4. Who drives this pole?

- The **negative pole** is clearly **human‑driven**: all high‑negative examples are human, and humans have the lowest mean on Factor 2.
- GPT texts (profiled and unprofiled) sit higher on Factor 2, so they are *less* associated with this negative‑pole style. This fits with LLMs typically producing more fluent, less disfluent, less taboo‑laden, and less “messy” conversational text.

So this pole captures a **distinctively human, messy, colloquial narrative mode** that GPT outputs under both prompts tend to under‑produce.

---

## 5. Proposed labels for this negative pole

Each label foregrounds a slightly different aspect, but all are consistent with the evidence:

1. **“Informal male‑centred anecdotal narrative”**  
   - Captures:  
     - Informal style (um, oh, shit, awful, alright)  
     - Male focus (he, him)  
     - Anecdotal, story‑like structure with time anchoring (minute).

2. **“Spontaneous, time‑anchored storytelling about him”**  
   - Emphasises:  
     - Spontaneity and disfluency (um, oh, alright, repetitions)  
     - Fine‑grained temporal framing (minute)  
     - Third‑person male protagonist (he, him).

3. **“Colloquial, evaluative narratives of everyday male behaviour”**  
   - Highlights:  
     - Colloquial lexis and mild taboo (shit, awful)  
     - Evaluative stance toward the male figure (malicious, violent, wicked, needs to grow up)  
     - Everyday, relational scenarios (work, school, nights out, childcare).

Any of these would be a defensible label for this pole. If a single concise label is needed, I would choose:

> **Informal male‑centred anecdotal narrative**

because it integrates the key dimensions indicated by both the loadings and the examples:
- **Informal**: disfluencies, fillers, taboo, stance markers.  
- **Male‑centred**: dense use of he/him as story focus.  
- **Anecdotal narrative**: short, time‑anchored episodes told as personal stories.
