Interpretation of Factor 5 (negative pole)

1. Which sources drive this pole?

- Mean scores:
  - Human: 0.56  
  - Profiled GPT: 0.34  
  - Unprofiled GPT: 0.32  

Factor scores are *overall positive* for all three groups, so the **negative pole is relatively under‑represented** across the corpus. No group has a negative mean, but humans are furthest from this pole (highest positive mean), and both GPT conditions are somewhat closer to it. Within the negative exemplars, all 10 are **unprofiled GPT**, so in practice this pole is **driven by unprofiled GPT outputs**.

So: this is a relatively marginal discourse style in the corpus, most clearly realised in unprofiled GPT turns.

2. Linguistic loadings

Negative pole loadings (strongest to weakest):

- um (−.46)
- normal (−.38)
- explain (−.36)
- themselves (−.34)
- chat (−.33)
- push (−.32)

These items cluster around:

- **Meta‑discourse and stance**: explain, chat  
- **Normativity / evaluation**: normal  
- **Reflexivity / self‑reference**: themselves  
- **Interpersonal pressure / resistance**: push  
- **Filled pause**: um

Taken together, they suggest a discourse where speakers:

- Mark **explanation** explicitly (“explain”, “I’ll explain”, “he explains”).
- Contrast events with what is **“normal”**, invoking norms or expectations.
- Refer to people reflexively (“themselves”) in ways that often highlight agency, responsibility, or self‑treatment.
- Describe **pushing** (social pressure, resistance, or assertiveness).
- Potentially include **hesitation** (“um”), signalling planning or delicacy (though “um” doesn’t appear in the top 10 examples, it is important corpus‑wide).

3. What do the examples show?

All examples are narrative/retrospective accounts of interpersonal situations, but with a particular flavour. I’ll highlight how the loading words function.

a) normal, explain – re‑framing behaviour against norms

- Woolpit story: “she started to eat normal food… once she could talk properly she explained…”  
  - “normal food” contrasts an earlier, marked state; “explained” frames a retrospective rationalisation of a strange event.
- Car park incident: “the poor girl was trying to explain… that kind of behaviour just seems completely normal to her.”  
  - “explain” is about getting one’s side heard; “normal” explicitly invokes a **normative frame** (what should/shouldn’t be normal in a family).

b) explain, push – effortful disclosure and pressure

- Badminton/CRB story: “he sort of pushes that a bit… explaining everything…”  
  - “pushes” = someone actively promoting an option; “explaining” = detailed procedural justification.
- Driving ban confession: “He explains that before… he’s pushing himself to keep going…”  
  - “explains” = careful, stepwise disclosure; “pushing himself” = emotional effort to reveal uncomfortable truth.

c) normal – contrastive evaluation

- Massage story: “a completely different feeling from a normal massage”  
  - “normal” again marks a **baseline expectation** against which the narrated experience is evaluated.

d) explain – rationalising and making sense

- Consumer rights dispute: “explained the whole thing… proper legal‑sounding… by the book.”  
  - “explained” introduces a structured, quasi‑legal narrative of events and rights.
- Funeral/jealousy: “I honestly can’t even explain how strange it felt…”  
  - Here “explain” marks the **limits of explanation**, but still foregrounds the attempt to make sense of an interpersonal moment.

e) push – conflict, resistance, and pressure

- Festival/obsessive guy: “trying to push past”  
  - Physical/social boundary‑crossing; the narrator’s friend intervenes.
- Housing association: “he pushed it really hard. I had to step in and say, look, you can’t just evict them like that”  
  - Institutional pressure vs. ethical/legal resistance.
- Paris trip: “I pushed back a bit because it was such an outdated thing to say”  
  - Assertive disagreement in a friendship context.

Across these texts, the loading items co‑occur with:

- **Detailed recounting of interpersonal or institutional conflicts**.
- Frequent **justification, rationalisation, or explanation of actions**.
- Explicit **evaluation against norms** (“normal”, “over the top”, “ridiculous”, “illegal”, “by the book”).
- Descriptions of **pressure, boundary‑pushing, and resistance** (“pushes that a bit”, “pushing himself”, “pushed it really hard”, “pushed back”).

4. Integrating loadings and examples

Giving equal weight to loadings and examples:

- The loadings point to a **meta‑explanatory, norm‑oriented, pressure‑laden discourse**.
- The examples instantiate this as:
  - Narratives where speakers or characters **explain themselves or others**.
  - Strong orientation to **what is normal / acceptable / legal / by the book**.
  - Recurrent **tension, conflict, or pressure**, often with someone “pushing” and someone resisting or needing to be heard.
  - A sense of **retrospective sense‑making**: people look back, explain, justify, or fail to explain.

The presence of “um” and “chat” in the loadings (though not in these 10 excerpts) suggests that, corpus‑wide, this pole also includes more **hesitant, conversationally marked explanation** (“um, let me explain”, “in this chat we were trying to…”), reinforcing the idea of on‑the‑spot sense‑making and negotiation.

5. Who is driving this pole?

- All high‑scoring negative examples are **unprofiled GPT**.
- Humans have the **highest positive mean**, so they are furthest from this negative pole.
- Profiled GPT is intermediate.

So this discourse style—high in “explain/normal/push/themselves/chat/um”—is **most characteristic of unprofiled GPT outputs** in this dataset. It looks like the unprofiled model tends to produce elaborated, norm‑referencing, explanatory narratives with explicit markers like “explain” and “normal”, and with stylised depictions of “pushing” and resistance.

6. Proposed labels for this pole

Focusing on this pole alone (no versus label), and capturing both the lexical pattern and the discourse function, plausible labels include:

1. **Norm‑Oriented Explanatory Conflict Narration**  
   - “Norm‑oriented”: captures “normal” and the frequent appeal to what is acceptable, legal, or expected.  
   - “Explanatory”: foregrounds “explain” and the meta‑discourse of justifying and making sense.  
   - “Conflict narration”: reflects “push” and the recurrent interpersonal/institutional tensions.

2. **Justificatory, Norm‑Evaluative Storytelling**  
   - “Justificatory”: many speakers are explaining or defending actions (their own or others’).  
   - “Norm‑evaluative”: explicit contrasts with “normal”, “over the top”, “ridiculous”, “illegal”.  
   - “Storytelling”: all examples are extended narratives of events.

3. **Pressure and Explanation in Everyday Narratives**  
   - “Pressure”: from “push” and descriptions of being pushed, pushing back, or pushing oneself.  
   - “Explanation”: from “explain” and the broader pattern of rationalisation.  
   - “Everyday narratives”: these are ordinary life stories (school trips, driving bans, housing, friendships, massages).

Any of these captures the same underlying dimension:

- A discourse style where speakers **recount interpersonal or institutional situations by explicitly explaining, justifying, and evaluating them against perceived norms, often in the context of pressure, conflict, or boundary‑pushing**—a style particularly characteristic of unprofiled GPT turns in this corpus.
